# Insight catalogue — Profile dynamics (engine 2.0.0, catalogue 2.0.0)

_Generated by `node tools/gen-docs.js` from `intelligence-engine.js`. Do not edit by hand._

## How a rule fires
- A facet is **high** at 62+ and **low** at 42 or below. Anything between is the middle range, where a rule stays silent (no filler text).
- A rule has four defined states (hi-hi, hi-lo, lo-hi, lo-lo). Each state has its own interpretation and a set of signed *claims* used to detect contradictions between surfaced insights.
- Confidence measures support for the *inference*, not how strong the trait is. It is capped at "limited" when a score is within 3 points of a cut-off, when answers behind a facet disagree, when questions were skipped, or when answer-level evidence is missing. It is never "high" (two items per facet, no validation study).
- The engine abstains entirely for straight-lined responses or more than 7 skipped questions.
- Budget: up to 3 insights shown, 2 more on request; one per theme; a facet is used at most twice.
- Every generated string is linted for diagnostic or deterministic language before it can be shown.

## Vision translated into scale — `vision-to-scale`

- **Theme:** scope
- **Facets:** Abstraction (`P.abstraction`) × Ambition (`M.ambition`)
- **Rationale:** Abstract thinking sets how large a picture someone naturally sees; ambition sets how large a goal they choose. Whether the two rise together shapes whether ideas become projects.
- **Counterexample:** A person with high abstraction and high ambition who works in a role with no room for either will not show this pattern in their behaviour.
- **Limits:** Two items per facet; a self-report of how you see yourself, not a record of what you have built.
- **Other explanations:**
  - Your current role may reward practical delivery over big-picture thinking regardless of preference.
  - Ambition scores can reflect current circumstances more than a lasting orientation.
- **Experiment (1 week):** Choose one idea and write a one-page path from concept to a measurable first outcome.
- **Success signal:** You have the one-pager and have either completed its first step or consciously dropped it.

| State | Claims | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| hi-hi | builds-systems:+ | Conceptual thinking and large-scale ambition reinforce each other. You may be drawn to building a system or future, not just completing an isolated task. |
| hi-lo | builds-systems:- | Conceptual range is stronger than appetite for scale. You may generate more ideas than you choose to pursue at scale; notice whether that is a genuine preference for contained goals or goals you have not yet allowed yourself to want. |
| lo-hi | builds-systems:+ | Large goals are paired with a more concrete, practical thinking style. You may pursue scale through steps and proof rather than theory; check whether stepping back to frame the whole picture would surface options earlier. |
| lo-lo | builds-systems:- | A concrete thinking style and a modest appetite for scale appear together. That can favour well-defined, achievable work; the thing to check is whether “enough” is a choice or a default. |

## Exploration versus completion — `explore-finish`

- **Theme:** finishing
- **Facets:** Curiosity (`P.curiosity`) × Persistence (`M.persistence`)
- **Rationale:** Curiosity pulls attention toward new questions; persistence keeps attention on a chosen one. The balance between them shapes how projects end.
- **Counterexample:** A curious person with a structured team and clear milestones may finish consistently despite a low persistence score.
- **Limits:** Persistence is rated in general; it can differ a lot by domain.
- **Other explanations:**
  - Deadlines, accountability and how interesting the work is can matter more than either trait.
  - Persistence on work you care about can differ sharply from persistence on obligations.
- **Experiment (1 week):** Keep a “later ideas” list and finish one defined milestone before switching projects.
- **Success signal:** One milestone finished and no more than the planned number of new projects started.

| State | Claims | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| hi-lo | finishes-projects:- | Curiosity is more pronounced than persistence in this profile. New questions may feel more rewarding than the final stretch of an existing project; this is a pattern to check against your actual history. |
| lo-hi | finishes-projects:+ | Persistence is more pronounced than curiosity. You may be good at staying with a chosen path, while needing to deliberately make room for fresh information or alternatives. |
| hi-hi | finishes-projects:+ | Curiosity and persistence both score strongly. You may be able to explore widely and still carry chosen work through; the pressure point is deciding what not to explore while a commitment is live. |
| lo-lo | finishes-projects:- | Neither curiosity nor persistence stands out. Open-ended efforts may lose momentum; short, clearly defined commitments could suit you better than long, loosely framed ones. |

## Speed and risk appetite — `pace-risk`

- **Theme:** decisions
- **Facets:** Tempo (`R.tempo`) × Risk (`R.risk`)
- **Rationale:** Decision pace and tolerance for downside combine into a decision style; the mismatch cases (fast but cautious, slow but bold) are often the most informative.
- **Counterexample:** Someone with a fast pace and high risk appetite who has been burned recently may be deliberately slower right now.
- **Limits:** Describes a general tendency; real decisions vary by stakes, time pressure and information.
- **Other explanations:**
  - Stakes and reversibility change both pace and risk-taking for almost everyone.
  - Organisational culture can impose a pace that differs from your preference.
- **Experiment (next significant decision):** Before one consequential decision, record the upside, worst plausible downside, reversibility and deadline.
- **Success signal:** You can say, afterwards, whether the written downside matched what happened.

| State | Claims | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| hi-hi | moves-fast:+, takes-risk:+ | Quick decisions and bold risk-taking appear together. This can create momentum; the key safeguard is a brief downside check before decisions that are costly to reverse. |
| lo-lo | moves-fast:-, takes-risk:- | A measured pace and downside protection appear together. This can support careful judgment; time-boxing deliberation may help when a good-enough decision is reversible. |
| hi-lo | moves-fast:+, takes-risk:- | A quick pace is paired with lower risk appetite. You may decide fast on familiar matters but hold back when the downside is large; compare this with specific decisions you have made. |
| lo-hi | moves-fast:-, takes-risk:+ | A deliberate pace is paired with higher risk appetite. You may take time to decide but commit firmly to a bold option once you have; watch for deliberation that substitutes for acting. |

## Conviction tested by curiosity — `conviction-curiosity`

- **Theme:** beliefs
- **Facets:** Conviction (`I.conviction`) × Curiosity (`P.curiosity`)
- **Rationale:** Conviction settles a view; curiosity keeps testing it. Their balance affects how easily a position is revised.
- **Counterexample:** A person with high conviction who routinely seeks out disconfirming evidence may be more open than the score suggests.
- **Limits:** Measures stated firmness of views, not whether the views are well-founded.
- **Other explanations:**
  - Openness often depends on the topic and on who is in the room.
  - Strong conviction in one area does not imply the same everywhere.
- **Experiment (1 week):** Pick one belief and write both the strongest supporting evidence and the strongest evidence against it.
- **Success signal:** You have both lists, and can state what evidence would change your mind.

| State | Claims | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| hi-hi | revises-views:+ | Strong conviction coexists with strong curiosity. This can support independent thinking when curiosity keeps conviction open to evidence rather than turning it into a fixed position. |
| hi-lo | revises-views:- | Conviction is stronger than exploratory pull in these scores. When a belief matters to you, deliberately seeking the strongest counter-evidence may protect against premature closure. |
| lo-hi | revises-views:+ | Curiosity is stronger than attachment to a settled position. You may find it natural to keep exploring; writing down a provisional conclusion can help convert exploration into decisions. |
| lo-lo | revises-views:+ | Views are held lightly and there is little pull to dig into them. Questions may stay open by default; decide which ones deserve a conclusion. |

## Structure and adaptability — `structure-flex`

- **Theme:** planning
- **Facets:** Discipline (`M.discipline`) × Adaptability (`R.adaptability`)
- **Rationale:** Discipline favours plans and routine; adaptability favours changing course. Having both, one, or neither changes how work feels under change.
- **Counterexample:** A highly disciplined person who has built a flexible system may adapt easily despite a lower adaptability score.
- **Limits:** General preference; not a measure of how well plans are actually executed.
- **Other explanations:**
  - Task type matters: many people are structured at work and flexible elsewhere.
  - Workload and deadlines change how much structure feels helpful.
- **Experiment (1 week):** Use a three-item checklist for a routine task, while leaving one planned slot open for changes.
- **Success signal:** The checklist caught at least one detail, and the open slot was used or consciously released.

| State | Claims | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| hi-hi | follows-plans:+, changes-course:+ | Structure and adaptability both score strongly. You may be able to use plans as scaffolding without treating every change as a failure of the plan. |
| hi-lo | follows-plans:+, changes-course:- | Structure is stronger than adaptability in these scores. Plans may help you execute, but an explicit review point can prevent commitment to a plan after its assumptions have changed. |
| lo-hi | follows-plans:-, changes-course:+ | Adaptability is stronger than preference for structure. Improvisation may be comfortable; a small external checklist can protect details when work becomes repetitive. |
| lo-lo | follows-plans:-, changes-course:- | Neither firm structure nor quick adaptability stands out. Work may feel hardest when it needs either a fixed plan or a fast change of direction; one light routine you can rely on may help. |

## Empathy and interpersonal boundaries — `empathy-boundary`

- **Theme:** disagreement
- **Facets:** Empathy (`S.empathy`) × Harmony (`S.harmony`)
- **Rationale:** Empathy reads other people’s feelings; harmony-seeking decides how much of a disagreement is voiced. Together they shape how conflict is handled.
- **Counterexample:** Someone with high harmony-seeking in general may be very direct with a few trusted people.
- **Limits:** Self-perceived empathy is not the same as accuracy in reading others.
- **Other explanations:**
  - Close relationships and workplaces often call for different behaviour than your default.
  - Strong empathy can coexist with either avoiding or voicing conflict, depending on the relationship.
- **Experiment (next conversation where you disagree):** In one conversation, state your view clearly and ask the other person to describe theirs in their own words.
- **Success signal:** You said your view in the first few sentences and the other person confirmed you understood theirs.

| State | Claims | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| hi-hi | voices-disagreement:- | Emotional attunement and harmony-seeking both score strongly. These can support considerate relationships; the counterweight is stating a real disagreement before resentment builds. |
| hi-lo | voices-disagreement:+ | Empathy is stronger than harmony-seeking. You may notice other people’s feelings while still being willing to name disagreement; check whether your delivery matches your intention. |
| lo-hi | voices-disagreement:- | Harmony-seeking is stronger than emotional attunement in these scores. You may prioritise smooth interaction without necessarily sensing what another person feels, so asking directly can be more reliable than guessing. |
| lo-lo | voices-disagreement:+ | Direct, matter-of-fact interaction may come easily. Check that your intent is received as intended, since others can read candour as indifference. |

## Independence and social alignment — `independence-belonging`

- **Theme:** disagreement
- **Facets:** Autonomy (`I.autonomy`) × Harmony (`S.harmony`)
- **Rationale:** Autonomy is wanting to decide for yourself; harmony-seeking is wanting agreement. Their balance shapes how decisions are made with other people.
- **Counterexample:** A person with low autonomy who has strong expertise may decide independently within that area.
- **Limits:** General tendency; hierarchy, culture and stakes override it.
- **Other explanations:**
  - Power dynamics and who you are deciding with change this a lot.
  - High autonomy at work and high deference at home (or the reverse) is common.
- **Experiment (1 week):** Before agreeing to a request, privately write your preference, then decide what you are willing to compromise on.
- **Success signal:** You wrote your preference first at least twice and can say whether the final agreement matched it.

| State | Claims | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| hi-hi | defers-to-group:- | Independent judgment coexists with a preference for harmony. You may want to decide for yourself while preserving a workable relationship; clear, respectful disagreement can serve both. |
| hi-lo | defers-to-group:- | Independent judgment is paired with a lower harmony score. Direct challenge may feel natural; check whether the goal is to improve the decision or simply to defend autonomy. |
| lo-hi | defers-to-group:+ | Harmony-seeking is stronger than independent judgment in these scores. Before agreeing, pause long enough to identify what you actually think and whether the agreement is freely chosen. |
| lo-lo | defers-to-group:+ | Neither independent judgment nor smoothing things over stands out. You may be influenced by whoever is most decisive in the room; naming your own preference first can keep it visible. |

## Ambition and sustained execution — `ambition-execution`

- **Theme:** goals
- **Facets:** Ambition (`M.ambition`) × Persistence (`M.persistence`)
- **Rationale:** Ambition sets the size of goals; persistence determines whether effort survives the unglamorous middle. The pair predicts where goals tend to stall, as a hypothesis.
- **Counterexample:** A person with high ambition and low persistence working with strong accountability may execute reliably.
- **Limits:** Describes tendencies, not track record. Compare with completed work.
- **Other explanations:**
  - Support, resources and how a goal was chosen affect execution at least as much as either trait.
  - Persistence for chosen goals can differ from persistence for assigned ones.
- **Experiment (1 week):** Define one outcome for the next seven days and a visible “done” condition before starting.
- **Success signal:** The outcome was either done by the stated condition or you can name what changed.

| State | Claims | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| hi-hi | sustains-effort:+ | Large goals and persistence reinforce each other in the score pattern. The practical question is whether your calendar and completed work reflect the scale of your intentions. |
| hi-lo | sustains-effort:- | Ambition is more pronounced than persistence. Smaller milestones, visible completion criteria and short feedback loops may help large goals survive the less exciting middle. |
| lo-hi | sustains-effort:+ | Persistence is more pronounced than ambition. You may sustain effort reliably once a goal is chosen; periodically checking whether the goal is still worth the effort keeps execution purposeful. |
| lo-lo | sustains-effort:- | Lower ambition and lower persistence appear together. A low-pressure pace may suit you; check that it matches what you want from the next year rather than avoiding effortful goals by default. |
